Not long ago, I was pairing with our social media lead, trying to understand how I could collaborate and learn from their workflow. There was one moment during the session that has kept resonating with me.

The moment they said:

"AH HA... so THIS is how they say you use AI at work."

It was a genuine reaction. A realization of what using AI at the next level actually looks like.

At Go Champs, every month we publish a series on Instagram: the Top 5 records of the month on our platform. Simple concept, tedious execution.

Someone pulls the data from the database. Someone else copies the card template in Canva five times and manually updates the names, numbers, and stats to match. Two people, four to five hours, every single month, just to ship five cards.

Our social media lead had already used AI to brainstorm the series concept and help design the Canva template itself. And that's great as a starting point. But we never really stopped to ask how we could automate part of this monthly routine.

So, while pairing with them, I started looking for parts of their workflow where a small set of tools could do the job.

We created a Canva template designed to be filled programmatically, a couple of AI skills, and a playbook tying them together. On top of that, we built a small agent connected via MCP that can run the database queries and push the results directly into the Top 5 Canva cards.

And this is actually what I think the future of our job looks like...

__Teach AI to do the manual and repetitive work.__

After putting all of this together, I asked them to tell the agent:

"Generate the Top 5."

The agent started thinking... One minute... Two... and boom!

Our Top 5 cards were filled with all the information we needed.

What used to take two people four to five hours now takes one to two minutes. That's not a productivity tweak. That's a completely different shape of work.

Why the reaction mattered more than the automation

Here's the part I keep coming back to. The technical build — queries, templates, MCP integration — is the kind of work engineers do all the time. Not trivial, but familiar. What was new was watching someone outside engineering see the result and immediately recognize what it meant, out loud, with that specific mix of amusement and disbelief.

That reaction is a signal. Early AI adoption looks like curiosity — "let's see what it can draft." Then it looks like utility — reaching for it on specific tasks, like template ideas or a first-pass caption. What we hit with the Top 5 series is a third stage: AI absorbing an entire workflow end to end, data to publish-ready asset, with no human in the loop except the person asking for it.

The "aha" moment is what happens when someone who lived through stage one and two suddenly sees stage three land on their own desk. It's not disbelief that AI can do this. It's the recognition that the gap between "AI helps me work" and "AI does this part of the work" just closed, quietly, while everyone was focused on something else.

The real shift: from assistant to operator

Using AI to brainstorm a caption or a template is AI as an assistant — useful, but bounded to a single step you still own. What we built for the Top 5 series is different: AI as an operator inside a process, with its own tools, its own connection to real data, and a defined job to finish, not just a suggestion to offer.

That distinction is, I think, the actual maturity curve people are on right now. Not "do you use AI" — almost everyone does, somewhere. The real question is whether AI is still confined to the parts of the work you personally supervise step by step, or whether it's been handed an entire outcome to own.

What I'd tell other teams

Don't start by trying to automate the whole thing. We didn't. AI helped with the template and the ideas long before it touched the database. The end-to-end version only became possible because the smaller uses came first — they're what surfaced the actual bottleneck (the manual bridge, not the creative work) and gave us a template and a process worth wiring an agent into.

If a repetitive, well-defined piece of your work still runs on two people and a few hours every month, that's usually a sign the pieces already exist — data source, template, process — and just haven't been connected yet.

Where this leaves us

Our Top 5 series still has a person behind it — someone who decides to run it, checks the result, posts it. That's not going away, and I don't think it should. What changed is everything between the decision and the outcome.

If you've had your own version of that "ha ha, so THIS is how we use AI now" moment recently, take it as a marker, not a milestone to chase. It means some piece of your work quietly crossed from AI-assisted to AI-operated. The interesting question isn't whether that's impressive — it's whether you know exactly which piece it was, and whether it deserves to stay that way.
